What you're seeing

  1. Gravity pulls you and the laser straight down at about three times Earth's pull (30 blocks/s², 1 block = 1 m), for snappy jumps and falls. Real light falls far too little to see, so here the laser falls as if it were fired at 20 blocks/s: a level shot starts curving down along a circle of radius about 13 blocks and lands about 6 blocks away from standing height. The slider makes it stronger, weaker or off. It never slows the beam, only turns it, so a shot aimed up rises, curves over and ends up falling straight down.
  2. Black holes still bend the beam as exactly as before, on top of the downward pull. Fire the fan near a hole and the arcs dive into it or swing around it. The beam is drawn where it really is: every pixel's ray checks how close it passes it, so you can see it behind a hole and inside the Einstein ring. The light you see is not pulled down, only bent by the holes, so the world looks the same as in the other demo.
  3. Every pixel is a bent light ray. Rays are traced backwards from your eye along real Schwarzschild light paths, so straight lines behind a hole (the checkerboard, the wall, the pillars) curve around it.
  4. The shadow is about 2.6× the horizon radius, not 1×. Light that comes within 1.5 rs (the photon sphere) spirals in. Just outside it, a thin bright Einstein ring shows a distorted image of the whole scene behind you. Aim a laser just past the shadow's edge and it whips around the hole.
  5. Redshift. Blocks close to a hole look dimmer and redder, because their light loses energy climbing out. Fly close and the outside world turns brighter and bluer instead.
  6. Throw a ball (T) past a hole. It gets stretched along the line to the hole (tides), slows down, reddens and fades at the horizon. That's what a distant observer would actually see, rather than it simply vanishing.
  7. Accretion disk (G): gas orbiting at up to half the speed of light. You can see its far side above and below the shadow, and the side moving toward you is brighter and bluer (Doppler beaming).
  8. Place several holes. Each one lenses the images in the others. Emitters keep firing as you add, move or resize holes.
Physics choices: light bending is exact general relativity for a single hole; for several holes their effects are added together (an approximation). Lasers stop when they hit a block, cross a horizon, or have travelled 600 blocks. Black holes don't pull on you (only the downward gravity does), so you can walk right up to one. Ball gravity is weakened so you can watch balls fall: a real hole this size would weigh about 50 Earths. Tidal stretching of balls is exaggerated for visibility.
